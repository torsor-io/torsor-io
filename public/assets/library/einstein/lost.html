Modular relativity from relativity entropy

1 Abstract

We take an algebraic voyage suggested by Einstein's late unease with the continuum, starting with a von Neumann algebra of observables and exploring the ways in which a spacetime structure analogous to special relativity can emerge. In particular, we identify time with modular flow (in line with Connes and Rovelli's Thermal Time Hypothesis), space with the commutative shadow of an observer-dependent subalgebra, and observers themselves with cyclic, separating states. Comparisons between observers are governed by the Connes cocycle, with the real-time cocycle near \(t=0\) yielding relative entropy \(S\), interpreted as a comparison between modular “clocks”, and the Radon-Nikodym point \(t = -i/2\) giving a fidelity functional \(\mathcal{F}\) that we interpreted as a comparison of modular “rods”. In a Type I Gibbs model, these quantities admit a closed form relation \(\mathcal{F}^2 = e^{-S}\) in the well-localized (single eigenvalue) limit. We then analyze the Bisognano–Wichmann wedge, where the vacuum modular Hamiltonian generates Lorentz boosts, and discuss localized coherent excitations for which relative entropy is computable in terms of boost energy and the cocycle admits Gaussian closed forms at the Radon-Nikodym point. In this regime, special-relativistic kinematics emerges from modular data: rapidity appears as a localized relative-modular spectral parameter, while dispersion produces intrinsic “quantum” departures from rigid Lorentz scaling.

2 Introduction

One can give good reasons why reality cannot at all be represented by a continuous field. From the quantum phenomena it appears to follow with certainty that a finite system of finite energy can be completely described by a finite set of numbers (quantum numbers). This does not seem to be in accordance with a continuum theory and must lead to an attempt to find a purely algebraic theory for the representation of reality. But nobody knows how to find the basis for such a theory.

This paragraph, appearing in the final appendix of Einstein's The Meaning of Relativity (fifth edition, 1955), is perhaps the last thing the great scientist committed to print. It reflects a deep pessimism about the use of the continuum in modeling a quantum-mechanical reality, and more than a whiff of despair about the unified field theory he had been labouring over for decades. Written a few months before he died, it's tempting to frame this as a jeremiad of defeat. Maybe so. But it has a specificity suggesting Einstein knows more than he is saying; that perhaps he has been considering the very “algebraic theory” he decries as unknown or unknowable. We are not talking about the mature fruit of scientific work; that he would have published. But the man who spent so many hours wandering the byways of Princeton, lost in thought, might have devoted some of those hours to an algebraic approach to reality. To borrow an image of Grothendieck, “The unknown thing to be known appeared to me as some stretch of earth or hard marl, resisting penetration... The sea advances insensibly and in silence, nothing seems to happen, nothing moves, the water is so far off you hardly hear it... yet it finally surrounds the resistant substance.” From Récoltes et Semailles (1986). Einstein's sea might have been rising to insensibly to surround it.

Let us suppose, then, that Einstein spent some of those Princeton rambles following his own hint, immersed in (or immersing) the problem of replacing the continuum in accord with the facts of quantum mechanics. What might it look like? The most obvious place to start would have been the work of Irving Segal, a former postdoc of Einstein's at the IAS, or his Princeton colleague John von Neumann, both of whom approached algebra from the viewpoint of quantum-mechanical foundations. “Irreducible representations of operator algebras” and “Postulates for General Quantum Mechanics” (1947), Irving Segal; “Rings of Operators I” (1936) and “Rings of Operators II” (1937), Murray and von Neumann. Von Neumann (and his colleague Francis Murray) had defined objects called ““rings of operators”, later to be known as W*-algebras or simply von Neumann algebras; Segal generalized these to a broader class of C*-algebras. The literature was (and is) highly technical, and one can easily imagine Einstein, fed up with mathematical jargon, concluding he was too old to learn new tricks and retreating to the terra firma of his unified field theory.

But it is possible things went differently. Von Neumann was still at Princeton and dwelling on old flames; his 1954 ICM address on unsolved problems “Unsolved Problems in Mathematics" (1954), International Congress of Mathematics. The “soup” comment comes to us from Freeman Dyson via George Dyson, Turing's Cathedral (2012). exclusively concerned technical, physical, and logical/probabilistic aspects of von Neumann algebras. To the assembled mathematicians, it seemed like “warmed-up soup”, but it may have reflected genuine mathematical recidivism rather than laziness. It is not impossible that Einstein and von Neumann crossed paths in New Jersey in 1954; and that, amidst the Institute banter, careful avoidance of nuclear politics, and standard issue shop talk, they touched on a problem dear to both: the application of algebraic methods to a quantum-mechanical reality.

This essay is an attempt to honour this (perhaps) imaginary conversation between von Neumann and Einstein, and to trace out the few steps Einstein might have taken, armed with his delicate sense of physical truth, von Neumann's rings of operators, and (anachronistically) a slew of deep classification results that would not arrive until the late 60s and 70s. This anachronism is regrettably necessary because, without the specific form of these results, it is impossible to imagine Einstein taking interest in something as recherché as the taxonomy of infinite-dimensional algebras. These results make the math physically meaningful, and flesh out familiar physics mathematically. We now embark on Einstein's lost voyage.

3 Background

This section will outline standard algebraic background material, albeit in a slightly nontraditional way. For that reason, we do not recommend experts skip these preliminaries; they not only provide motivation, but ground some eccentricities of presentation.

3.1 Von Neumann algebras

A von Neumann algebra is a collection \(\mathfrak{M}\) of operators which, roughly speaking, correspond to measurements an observer can make, actions they can perform, or equivalently, transitions between states of the observer. This means that:

  • if \(a \in \mathfrak{M}\) and \(\lambda \in \mathbb{C}\), then \(\lambda a \in\mathfrak{M}\) since we can scale measurements;
  • if \(a, b \in \mathfrak{M}\), then \(a + b \in \mathfrak{M}\) since we can add measurements;
  • if \(a, b \in \mathfrak{M}\), then \(a\cdot b \in \mathfrak{M}\) since we can compose transformations;
  • there is an antilinear operator \(*: \mathfrak{M}\to \mathfrak{M}\) satisfying \((ab)^* = b^* a^*\), with \(a^*\) meaning we apply to the final state; and
  • there is a norm \(\Vert \cdot \Vert: \mathfrak{M}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\) satisfying \(\Vert a\Vert^2 = \lambda_\max \in \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\) is a norm, where \(\lambda_\max\) is the largest absolute eigenvalue of \(a^*a\), since measurements are finite.

So far, this defines a C*-algebra (once we supplement with standard axioms, e.g. \(\mathfrak{M}\) is a vector space, the product is associative, etc, I omit). The notion of a transition between states requires elaboration. Usually, a state \(\phi \in \mathfrak{M}^*_+\) is viewed as a linear functional \(\phi: \mathfrak{M} \to \mathbb{C}\) (written \(\phi \in \mathfrak{M}^*\)) which is positive, in that \(\phi(a^*a) \geq 0\) (written \(\phi \in \mathfrak{M}^*_+\)) and normalized so that \(\phi(e) = 1\), where \(e \in \mathfrak{M}\) is the identity. We denote Napier's constant by \(\text{e}\).

Given a state, we can build a Hilbert space as follows: define the form \(\langle a, b\rangle = \phi(a^* b)\), quotient out the null space \(\mathscr{N}_\phi\), and define \(\mathscr{H}_\phi\) as the vector space formed by equivalence classes \(\mathfrak{M}/\mathscr{N}_\phi\), completed with respect to the inner product \[ \langle \psi|\omega\rangle = \langle [a]_\phi, [b]_\phi\rangle = \phi(a^* b) \tag{1} \] where the elements \(\psi = [a]_\phi, \omega = [b]_\phi \in \mathscr{H}_\phi\). If the state \(\phi\) is faithful, meaning that \(\phi(a) = 0\) implies \(a = 0\) and hence the null space is trivial \(\mathscr{N}_\phi = \{0\}\), the induced Hilbert space \(\mathscr{H}_\phi\) faithfully embeds \(\mathfrak{M}\subseteq \mathfrak{B}(\mathscr{H})_\phi\). We always think of a von Neumann algebra “concretely”, that is, as a subalgebra of bounded operators acting on some Hilbert space, \(\mathfrak{M}\subseteq \mathfrak{B}(\mathscr{H})\).

We can view the inner product \(\langle \psi|\omega\rangle\) as computing a transition amplitude from \(\omega\) to \(\psi\). This gives the concrete interpretation of \(a^*\) as applying \(a\) to the final state, since \[ \langle \psi |a|\omega\rangle = \langle \psi | a\omega \rangle, \quad \langle \psi |a^*|\omega\rangle = \langle a\psi |\omega\rangle. \tag{2} \] Now we come to the crux: in addition to the axioms above, a von Neumann is closed in the weak operator topology, Called “weak” since it assumes less than another standard operator topology, known in contrast as the “strong operator topology”. meaning that for any convergent sequence of operators \(a_n \to a\), the limit \[ \langle \psi | a_n |\omega\rangle \to \langle \psi |a |\omega\rangle \tag{3} \] for any pair of vectors \(\psi\) and \(\omega\). More plainly, transition amplitudes are a continuous function of the transition operator \(a\). Physically, this corresponds to the ability to approximate transition amplitudes, a reasonable constraint for observers limited to finite-precision measurements. For a thoroughgoing introduction to von Neumann algebras, see Fundamentals of the Theory of Operator Algebras: Advanced Theory (1997), Kadison and Ringrose.

3.2 Space

As a starting point for the representation of reality, it seems pretty bare bones. We have no space, no time, nothing to pin the theory to a suspect continuum; just a few axioms about observation thrown together. Whether these are the right axioms, or some other flavour of algebraic object, is a matter of debate. But we will see that the structure of von Neumann algebras allows for something remarkable: the emergence of space and time.

Let's start with space. Suppose for a moment that observables in \(\mathfrak{M}\) are labelled by location in time and space, \(a(\mathbf{x}, t)\). Two observables \(a(\mathbf{x}, t), b(\mathbf{x}', t)\) sharing the same time, but in different places, should not be able to influence each other, either as measurements or actions; this would correspond to instantaneous transmission of information. A robust feature of nature is that it is local, i.e. no instantaneous transmission allowed. In terms of transition amplitudes, this means that the order we apply them in should be immaterial, \[ \langle \psi| a(\mathbf{x}, t)b(\mathbf{x}', t)|\omega\rangle = \langle \psi| b(\mathbf{x}', t)a(\mathbf{x}, t)|\omega\rangle \tag{4} \] for any initial and final states, or equivalently, \(ab - ba = [a, b] = 0\). In words, \(a\) and \(b\) commute. On the other hand, if it is possible to send a message between two spacetime points \((\mathbf{x}, t)\) and \((\mathbf{x}', t')\), and it is possible for the observer to distinguish them, we can construct initial and final states such that \[ \langle \psi| a(\mathbf{x}, t)b(\mathbf{x}', t')|\omega\rangle \neq \langle \psi| b(\mathbf{x}', t')a(\mathbf{x}, t)|\omega\rangle, \tag{6} \] and hence \([a, b] \neq 0\). The final state simply records which operator was observed first!

We say that points which can message each other are timelike-separated and points that cannot are spacelike-separated. Operators at spacelike separated points commute. This provides a simple algebraic recipe for space: we consider a subalgebra \(\Sigma \subseteq \mathfrak{M}\) of commuting operators that cannot be enlarged, also called a maximal abelian subalgebra (masa). There is a subtlety regarding maximality with respect to which algebra we will resolve in §5. The operators do not give space as we usually understand it, that is, via coordinates. To induce coordinates, we use the Gelfand isomorphism. This associates a commutative C*-algebra \(\Sigma\) to its set of states, called in this context the spectrum \(X = \text{spec}(\Sigma) \subseteq \Sigma^*\). These give rise to coordinates in the sense that \(\Sigma\) can simply be viewed as continuous functions on \(X\), with \[ \Sigma \cong C^0(X) \tag{7} \] as a norm-preserving isomorphism. With respect to the uniform norm on functions. For details, see Introduction to Topology and Modern Analysis (1964), George Simmons. Concretely, the spaces are idenfied by the Gelfand map \(\Phi: \Sigma \to X\), whose seemingly innocuous definition \[ \Phi(a)(\phi) = \phi(a) \tag{8} \] underlies Fourier series (\(\Sigma = \mathbb{S}^1\)), the Fourier transform (\(\Sigma = \mathbb{C}\)), and the Mellin transform (\(\Sigma = \mathbb{R}^\times_{>0}\)) among others. For instance, on the circle \(\Sigma = \mathbb{S}^1 = [0, 2\pi)\) (with endpoints idenfified), the characters are the exponential maps \[ \phi_k(z) = \text{e}^{2\pi i kz} \tag{9} \] for \(k \in \mathbb{Z}\). Thus, we can view \(\text{spec}(\mathbb{S}^1) = \mathbb{Z}\) as the integers labelling the familiar Fourier modes on a circle.

So, to identify space, we therefore first identity a masa \(\Sigma\), and arrive at coordinates \(X = \text{spec}(\Sigma)\) given by the states on the algebra. The algebra will have projections \[ \Pi^2 = \Pi^* \Pi = \Pi \tag{10} \label{pi_1} \] which we can view as indicator functions on their support: \[ \text{supp}(\Pi) = \{x \in X : \Pi(x) \neq 0\}. \tag{11} \label{pi_2} \] This allows to talk of regions of space; there is no guarantee we can talk about points (it depends on the projection structure of the masa) so this notion of space is already non-local. Given the difficulty of covariantly specifying a point, i.e. without coordinates, this is a feature rather than a bug! Since the structure of a masa in a von Neumann algebra is highly constrained, we can say more. It turns out that masa only come in three basic flavours: “Extensions of pure states” (1959), Kadison and Singer.

  • \(X\) finite, with model space \(X \cong \{1, 2, \ldots, n\}\);
  • \(X\) infinite and discrete, with model space \(X \cong \mathbb{N}\); and
  • \(X\) continuous, with \(X \cong [0, 1]\).

We can also take direct sums of these basic types, so for instance a \(d\)-dimensional continuum corresponds to \(d\) copies of the third type. Only the first two allow us to project onto a point; the price of a continuum is non-locality. Einstein was right to be suspicious! So passes the glory of space.

3.3 Time

Space is built out of what observers cannot measure. In contrast, there is no natural way to define a maximal set of non-commuting operators; we will tend to get back $\mathfrak{M}$! Time also has the puzzling phenomenological feature that (like the Marcusian man) it is one-dimensional. Finally, there should be a connection between thermodynamics and temporal flow, if we are to believe Eddington's thesis on the Arrow of Time. The Nature of the Physical World (1928), Arthur Eddington. How can we reconcile these requirements with the algebraic approach?

Recall the interpretation of \(a^*\): it is \(a\) applied to a final state rather than an initial state. Thus, given a unit vector \(|\Omega\rangle\) in the (faithful) Hilbert space \(\mathscr{H}_\phi\), we already have a notion of earlier and later in time, and can ferret out asymmetries by considering how \(a^*\) affects the state in contrast to \(a\). To probe this, it is not enough to consider \[ \langle \psi|a |\psi\rangle \quad \text{vs} \quad \langle \psi|a^* |\psi\rangle, \tag{12} \] since these are complex conjugates and classical observers only have access to probabilities rather than amplitudes. Put differently, these quantities only differ by an unobservable global phase! The next simplest objects to consider are \[ \langle \psi|a^*a |\psi\rangle \quad \text{vs} \quad \langle \psi|a a^* |\psi\rangle. \tag{13} \] When \(a\) is self-adjoint (\(a^* = a\)) or normal (\(a^*a=aa^*\)) these are trivially related, but for a general operator there is no immediate connection. To see why this might be useful, consider the case of a harmonic oscillator with creation operator \(a^*\) and annihilation operator \(a\). Technically, this isn't quite a von Neumann algebra, but we shouldn't let technical niceties get in the way of a good story. The correct treatment uses the CCR algebra, taming unbounded ladder operators by exponentiation. First of all, \(N = a^*a\) is the number operator, and second, \(a\) and \(a^*\) anticommute, so the difference obeys \[ \langle \psi|a^*a |\psi\rangle - \langle \psi|aa^* |\psi\rangle = 2\langle N\rangle_\psi, \tag{14} \] i.e. is twice the average number of particles in state \(|\psi\rangle\). This example pleasingly illustrates that, in contrast to the commuting spatial case, time does appear to be tied to antcommutation, albeit between operators and their adjoints.

Let us try to capture the asymmetry more precisely. Consider the “adjoint swap” or modular operator \(\Delta_v\), defined by \[ \langle \psi| a^* \Delta_\psi b|\psi\rangle = \langle \psi| ab^* |\psi\rangle \tag{15} \] for all \(a, b \in \mathfrak{M}\). Let us specialize to the case where the unit vector \(|v\rangle\) is cyclic, meaning that \(\mathfrak{M}|\psi\rangle\) is dense in the Hilbert space \(\mathscr{H}\) that \(\mathfrak{M}\) acts on: \[ \overline{\mathfrak{M}|\psi\rangle} = \mathscr{H}. \tag{16} \] Then the modular operator is automatically positive, since \(|av\rangle\) is dense in the full Hilbert space \(\mathscr{H}\), and \[ \langle a\psi| \Delta_\psi|a\psi\rangle = \langle \psi|aa^*|\psi\rangle \geq 0, \tag{17} \] making tacit use of the weak closure property to densely approximate. Since \(\Delta_\psi\) is positive, it is diagonalizable with positive eigenvalues \(\text{e}^{-\lambda}\); We choose these so that λ can be arbitrary and real, and bounded below just in case the modular operator is bounded. these are precisely the operator asymmetries for the corresponding eigenvectors \(a_\lambda |\psi\rangle\). Thus, if the map \(\Delta_\psi\) exists, it tells us how wonky each operator is with respect to swapping initial and final states, or equivalently, the ability of a state to tell the time from measuring observables and their adjoints.

So far, we have only the discrete choice of initial or final state. From this binary, we would like to concoct a continuous, one-dimensional flow of time. This seems quixotic, but if \(\Delta_\psi\) is bounded, then the positive eigenvalues \(\text{e}^{-\lambda}\) are bounded below, and hence the \(\lambda\) are bounded below. This suggests we can view them as energy levels of a modular Hamiltonian \(K_\psi\) defined by \[ \Delta_\psi = \text{e}^{-K_\psi}. \tag{18} \] If \(|\psi\rangle\) is also separating, meaning \(a |\psi\rangle = 0\) implies \(a = 0\), then \(\lambda = \infty\) is not an eigenvalue and \(K_\psi\) is itself bounded; this will prove to be a useful technical assumption. Time evolution with respect to the modular Hamiltonian is given by a propagator \[ \text{e}^{-it K_\psi} = \Delta^{it}_\psi. \tag{19} \] Since the state is naturally fixed (this is all based on a choice of reference state \(|\psi\rangle\)), we instead consider the Heisenberg picture evolution of an observable: \[ a \overset{t}{\mapsto} \Delta^{-it}_\psi a \Delta^{it}_\psi = \sigma^t_\psi(a). \tag{20} \] Note that \(\Delta_\psi\) is generically not contained in \(\mathfrak{M}\), but rather operates on the larger Hilbert space \(\mathscr{H}\). However, Remarkably, a series of deep theorems proved by Tomita and championed by Takesaki “On canonical forms of von Neumann algebras” (1967), Minoru Tomita; Tomita's theory of modular Hilbert algebras and its applications (1970), Masamichi Takesaki. imply that, not only does this Heisenberg evolution stay inside the algebra, \(\sigma^t_\psi(\mathfrak{M}) \subseteq \mathfrak{M}\), but represents a \(1\)-parameter family of automorphisms of \(\mathfrak{M}\), i.e. \(\sigma^t_\psi: \mathfrak{M}\to \mathfrak{M}\) is an automorphism for any \(t\in\mathbb{R}\).

Under modular flow, observables do not “leak out” of \(\mathfrak{M}\) and into the ambient space of bounded operators \(\mathfrak{B}(\mathscr{H})\); instead, they are quietly rearranged, like unitary time evolution in ordinary quantum mechanics. This suggests that modular flow might be identified with physical time, a proposal called the Thermal Time Hypothesis first made by Connes and Rovelli. “Von Neumann algebra automorphisms and time-thermodynamics relation in generally covariant quantum theories” (1994), Alain Connes and Carlo Rovelli. Although this proposal has many merits, it is poor in concrete predictions. To learn what it means, we need to move into a bigger universe.

3.4 Observers

A lone observer makes for a dull cosmology. Introducing a second observer corresponds to introducing a second (cyclic, separating) state \(|\omega\rangle\). The relative modular operator \(\Delta_{\omega|\psi}\) is defined by \[ \langle \omega | a^* \Delta_{\omega|\psi} b |\omega\rangle = \langle \psi|a b^* |\psi\rangle, \tag{21} \] so it not only swaps adjoints, but changes the reference state. As a simple example, \(\Delta_{\omega|\psi}\) converts the expectation of a self-adjoint observable \(b^*=b\) in state \(|\omega\rangle\) to an expectation in state \(|\psi\rangle\): \[ \langle \omega | \Delta_{\omega|\psi} b |\omega\rangle = \langle \psi|b |\psi\rangle. \tag{22} \] Again, \(\Delta_{\omega|\psi}\) is positive with eigenvalues $ \text{e}-λ$ for bounded \(\lambda\), giving a bounded relative modular Hamiltonian \(K_{\omega|\psi} = -\log\Delta_{\omega|\psi}\). Morally, \(K_\psi\) captures the speed at which operators are moving when flipped relative to \(\psi\), while \(K_{\omega|\psi}\) captures how fast they are moving when flipped between states. If this sounds like nonsense, try substituting a large eigenvalue into the defining equation. It can be shown that the relative modular flow is identical to the modular flow induced by \(\omega\): \[ \sigma^t_{\omega|\psi}(a) = \Delta^{-it}_{\omega|\psi} a \Delta^{it}_{\omega|\psi} = \Delta^{-it}_{\omega} a \Delta^{it}_{\omega} = \sigma^t_{\omega}(a). \tag{23} \] Formally, we can transition from the flow \(\sigma^t_{\omega|\psi}=\sigma^t_{\omega}\) to the flow \(\sigma^t_{\psi}\) by conjugating with the Connes cocycle, defined by \[ [D\omega: D\psi]_t := \Delta^{-it}_{\omega|\psi}\Delta^{it}_\psi. \tag{24}\label{cocycle} \] Both \(\Delta_{\omega|\psi}\) and \(\Delta_{\psi}\) live in the larger algebra \(\mathfrak{B}(\mathscr{H})\) rather than \(\mathfrak{M}\) itself, but remarkably, Connes showed that the parts outside \(\mathfrak{M}\) always cancel, so (\ref{cocycle}) is in \(\mathfrak{M}\). This result, called Connes' Cocycle Theorem, Noncommutative Geometry (1990), Alain Connes. is physically deep; it tells us that, for any two (suitably well-behaved) states, the modular flows they induce are equivalent up to an operator in \(\mathfrak{M}\). It's a bit like timelike trajectories, which may differ in their precise bearing, but always point into the forward lightcone.

This raises a question: if space is defined by a commuting algebra \(\Sigma\), are modular flows \(\sigma^t_\psi\) “timelike”, in the sense they point away from \(\Sigma\)? More precisely, do operators generically commute with their modular evolutions: \[ [a, \sigma^t_\psi(a)] \overset{?}{=} 0. \tag{25} \] A short calculation shows that \[ \frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t} [a, \sigma^t_\psi(a)]\big|_{t=0} = [a, [iK_\psi, a]], \tag{26} \] so commuting with modular flow means commuting with the modular Hamiltonian. In the finite-dimensional case, \(K_\psi\) is a matrix, and we can easily build non-commuting operators \(a\) which are not diagonal in the modular energy basis. This construction remains valid (with some bells and whistles) into infinite dimensions, so provided the modular Hamiltonian is not proportional to the identity \(K_\psi \propto e\), modular flows are timelike in the sense described.

By zooming in on \(t=0\), we just evaluated the initial “velocity vector” of a modular trajectory. We can perform a similar trick with two distinct observers, \(\psi\) and \(\omega\), albeit with respect to the cocycle. If \(\psi = \omega\), then \[ [D \omega: D\psi]_t = \Delta^{-it}_{\omega|\psi}\Delta^{it}_\psi = \Delta^{-it}_{\psi}\Delta^{it}_\psi = e \tag{27} \] for all \(t\in\mathbb{R}\). We might hope that the departure from the identity at small \(t\) encodes the divergence between the flows of the two states. To convert an operator into a scalar, we can just plug it into one of the states, say \(\omega\). Differentiating and taking \(t=0\) gives the relative entropy \[ S(\omega \Vert \psi) = i\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\langle\omega| [D\omega : D\psi]_t |\omega\rangle \big|_{t=0} \tag{28} \] where the constant is chosen so that (by the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula) \[ S(\omega \Vert \psi) = i\langle\omega|\left(i \log\Delta_{\omega|\psi} - i\log\Delta_\psi\right)|\omega\rangle. \tag{29}\label{relent1} \] Assuming that \(|\omega\rangle\) is normalized, the term \[ \langle \omega| \log\Delta_\omega|\omega\rangle = \log(\langle\omega|\Delta_\omega|\omega\rangle) =\log(\langle\omega|\omega\rangle) = 0 \tag{30} \] vanishes, leaving \(S(\omega \Vert \psi) = -\langle \omega | \log\Delta_{\omega|\psi} |\omega\rangle\). Substituting the real inequality \(\log x \leq x - 1\), we get an operator inequality with lower bound \[ S(\omega\Vert\psi) \geq 1 - \langle\omega | \Delta_{\omega|\psi}|\omega \rangle = 1 - \langle\psi|\psi\rangle = 0. \tag{31} \] It follows that relative entropy is always non-negative. With a little more work, one can show that \(S(\omega \Vert \psi) = 0\) just in case \(\omega = \psi\), so cocycle behaviour in the vicinity of \(t = 0\) precisely encodes when states are the same or different. One again, behaviour in the vicinity of \(t=0\) yields a telling “information displacement vector” which perfectly distinguishes observers.

4 Modular relativity

We now have the ingredients needed to start asking whether our algebraic definitions of space (a masa \(\Sigma \subseteq \mathfrak{M}\)), time (modular flow), and observers (cyclic, separating states) make sense. We will see that they can be coherently assembled into a theory of modular relativity, extending the Thermal Time Hypothesis from a single global modular flow, with reference to a mysteriously chosen universal state, to states-as-observers with compatibly timelike but locally different behaviour. The form of these local difference is highly constrained and suggests ways in which special and general relativity — Einstein's deepest contributions to physics — might emerge from the algebraic perspective.

4.1 Clocks

We will start by recasting the relative entropy in a suggestive form. We have been associating modular flow to Hamiltonians of various sorts, which interpret eigenvalues as scaling factors (for real time) or frequencies (for imaginary time). We can perform the same trick with the cocycle itself, although it has a slightly different structure. Define the cocycle Hamiltonian by \([D\omega: D\psi]_t = \text{e}^{-itH}\), so \[ \varphi(t) = \langle \omega | [D\omega: D\psi]_s |\omega \rangle = \langle \text{e}^{-itH}\rangle_\omega \tag{32} \] is the characteristic function of \(-H\) in state \(\omega\). Expanding in eigenfrequencies, we obtain \[ \varphi(t) = \int \text{e}^{-i\lambda t}\, \text{d}\mu(\lambda) = 1 - it\int \lambda \, \text{d}\mu(\lambda) + \mathcal{O}(t^2). \tag{33} \] By definition, \(\varphi'(0) = i S(\omega \Vert \psi)\), and hence we learn that relative entropy equals mean cocycle frequency: \[ S(\omega \Vert \psi) = \int \lambda \, \text{d}\mu(\lambda). \] Positivity of relative entropy Here, positivity is a euphimism for non-negativity. then implies an interesting fact: the mean cocycle frequency is always positive, and vanishes just in case the states coincide.

We can make this picture more physical using the notion of the Loschmidt echo. Named after (but not by) Johann Josef Loschmidt, who took Ludwig Boltzmann to task over issues of reversibility in classical physics. See “Dynamics of Loschmidt echoes and fidelity decay” (2006), Thomas Gorin, Tomaz Prosen, Thomas H. Seligman, and Marko Znidaric for a thorough review. In general, we can compare two Hamiltonians \(H_0\) and \(H_1\) on a reference state \(|\phi\rangle\) by evolving forward a time \(t\) with \(H_1\), then backward with \(H_0\) (or vice-versa), and taking the overlap with \(|\phi\rangle\): \[ \mathcal{L}_{H_0,H_1}^{\phi}(t) = \langle \phi|\text{e}^{itH_0}\text{e}^{-itH_1}|\phi\rangle. \tag{34} \] The difference between Hamiltonian spectra will manifest as dephasing, resulting in a loss of overlap. Typically, the focus is on the Loschdmit probability, i.e. the squared amplitude \(|\mathcal{L}_{H_0,H_1}^{\psi}(t)|^2\), which automatically cancels the first order term and is guaranteed to be non-increasing at second order because \(t = 0\) is a maximum: \[| \mathcal{L}_{H_0,H_1}^{\psi}(t)|^2 = 1 - t^2 |\Delta| + \mathcal{O}(t^4). \tag{35} \] We are more interested in the linear term, whose sign is not constrained in general. To understand what it signifies, consider a simple situation where \(|\psi\rangle\) is an eigenvector of the two Hamiltonians, with eigenvalue \(\lambda_0, \lambda_1\) respectively. Then \[ \mathcal{L}_{H_0,H_1}^{\psi}(t) = \text{e}^{-it(\lambda_1 - \lambda_0)} = 1 -it(\lambda_1 - \lambda_0) + \mathcal{O}(t^2), \tag{36} \] so the Loschdmit echo records the frequency difference at first order. In plain language, it tells us which Hamiltonian runs faster. In general, we can use Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff to write \[ \text{e}^{itH_0}\text{e}^{-itH_1} = \left(1 + \mathcal{O}(t^2)\right) \text{e}^{-it (H_1 - H_0)} = \left(1 + \mathcal{O}(t^2)\right) \int \text{e}^{-it\lambda}\,\text{d}\lambda, \tag{37} \] where we integrate over the spectrum of \(H_1 - H_0\). Expanding \(|\phi\rangle\) in eigenstates \(a_\lambda|\phi\rangle\) (taking \(|\phi\rangle\) cyclic), with coefficients \(c_\lambda\), gives \[ \mathcal{L}_{H_0,H_1}^{\psi}(t) = \left(1 + \mathcal{O}(t^2)\right) \int \text{e}^{-it\lambda}c_\lambda\langle\psi|a_\lambda|\psi\rangle\,\text{d}\lambda. \tag{38} \] This tells us which Hamiltonian runs faster on average, as measured by \(|\phi\rangle\) in the sense that it is weighted by \(\text{d}\mu(\lambda) = c_\lambda \langle \psi|a_\lambda|\psi\rangle \,\text{d}\lambda\), i.e. how much each eigenvector contributes \(|\phi\rangle\). For a first order term \(-it\Delta\), \(H_1\) runs faster for \(\Delta > 0\) and \(H_0\) runs faster for \(\Delta < 0\).

This leads us to a second interpretation of the characteristic function \(\varphi(t)\) as a Loschdmit echo between modular time evolution for states \(\omega\) and \(\psi\) respectively. In this case, we are evolving backward in time \(t\) first (due to our “physics-style” convention for conjugating operators) with reference state \(|\omega\rangle\), \(H_0 = K_{\psi}\) and \(H_1 = K_{\omega|\psi}\). Since \(\sigma^t_{\omega|\psi} = \sigma^t_\omega\), we can simplify by replacing \(K_{\omega|\psi}\) with \(K_{\omega}\) in the cocycle structure, which yields \[ \varphi(t) = \mathcal{L}^\omega_{K_{\omega},K_{\psi}}(-t). \tag{39} \] The fact we first evolve backwards implies that, for first order term \(-it\Delta\), \(K_{\psi}\) runs faster for \(\Delta < 0\) and \(K_{\omega}\) runs faster for \(\Delta > 0\). Positivity of relative entropy then implies a remarkable conclusion: a state \(|\omega\rangle\) measures its own modular evolution to run faster, on average, than any other state. This is what it means for the mean cocycle frequency to be positive. This should of course remind of something else: a clock always measures its own time to run faster than any clock moving relative to it, an effect known as time dilation. (We will see below that modular time dilation is not precisely analogous to time dilation, but the analogy is good enough for the moment.) As with regular time dilation, the puzzling symmetry of modular time dilation — which Hamiltonian really runs faster? — is resolved by the fact that measurements are performed with respect to a state. Observer \(|\omega\rangle\) measures \(|\psi\rangle\) to run slower, and vice versa, because measurement itself breaks the symmetry.

Time dilation is a cornerstone of Einstein's theory of special relativity. The existence of a modular analogue raises three natural questions:

  • What invariance grounds this modular time dilation principle, akin to the constancy of the speed of light?
  • In special relativity, time dilation implies length contraction. Is there a modular length contraction principle?
  • Finally, how does modular time dilation relate to special-relativistic time dilation?

We will answer these questions in turn. The first is the simplest. The speed of light can be measured by, say, bouncing a laser off a mirror. The invariance of \(c\) means that the result of this experiment is the same even when we asymmetrically boost the mirror into an inertial reference frame moving relative to us, or equivalently, we boost ourselves. These boosts are symmetries of the vacuum, so in effect, this says that if we asymmetrically apply a symmetry of the vacuum, experiments determining \(c\) are invariant. In the modular case, we treat the state \(|\psi\rangle\) as the vacuum. Instead a change of frame, we will have a \(*\)-automorphism \(\alpha: \mathfrak{M} \to \mathfrak{M}\) relabelling elements of the algebra; this is a symmetry of \(|\psi\rangle\) just in case \[ \psi \circ \alpha (a) = \langle \psi|\alpha(a)|\psi\rangle = \langle \psi|a|\psi\rangle = \psi(a). \tag{40} \label{auto-psi} \] Bouncing lasers off mirrors compares frames; in our case, we identify with a frame \(|\omega\rangle\) and “bounce off” the vacuum \(|\psi\rangle\). Instead of computing a speed, the simplest thing we can compute is the relative entropy itself, \(S(\omega \Vert \psi)\). Asymmetrically “boosting” ourselves means applying the automorphism \(\alpha\) to our frame. This gives \[ S(\omega\circ \alpha \Vert \psi) = S(\omega\circ \alpha \Vert \psi \circ \alpha), \tag{41} \] using the symmetry (\ref{auto-psi}). But applying an automorphism to both reference states cannot change the relative entropy; it relabels the eigenvectors of the cocycle Hamiltonian but leaves the spectrum invariant. Thus, we learn that relative entropy is the quantum information invariant that grounds time dilation.

4.2 Rods

Next, we turn to length contraction. This is an intrinsically trickier problem; clocks are localized so a comparison is direct, whereas volumes are spread out. Luckily, there is a beautiful analogue to the relative entropy story. Instead of evaluating the cocycle at real time \(t \in\mathbb{R}\), or equivalently, we look at a specific point in imaginary time \(t = -i/2\): \[ \Delta_{\text{RN}}^{1/2} = [D\omega:D\psi]_{-i/2} = \Delta_{\omega|\psi}^{-1/2}\Delta_{\psi}^{1/2}, \tag{42} \label{RN-point} \] where RN stands for Radon-Nikodym. This corresponds to an “imaginary” Loschmidt echo, probing the spatial rather than the temporal structure of the cocycle: \[ \mathcal{F}(\omega, \psi) = \mathcal{L}^\psi_{K_\omega,K_\psi}(i/2) = \int \text{e}^{-\lambda/2}\, \text{d}\mu(\lambda) + \text{corrections}, \tag{43} \label{RN-fid} \] where \(\text{d}\mu(\lambda)=c_\lambda\psi(a_\lambda)\) as above. Up to a series of corrections from Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff (suppressed when \([K_\omega,K_\psi]\) is small), this is just the weighted mean eigenvalue of \(\Delta_{\text{RN}}\). This may seem like an arbitrary place to look, but this is precisely the point that converts between spatial measures on a masa \(\Sigma\), so it is the natural object to evaluate! More precisely, given a function \(f \in C^0(\text{spec}(\Sigma), \mathbb{C}) \cong \Sigma\), the weight of the function in a state is simply the evaluation, e.g. \[ \psi(f) = \int_{\text{spec}(\Sigma)} f(x) \, \text{d}\mu_\psi(x). \tag{44} \] In particular, for the projector \(\Pi_R\) associated to a region \(R \subseteq \text{spec}(\Sigma)\), the volume of the region is simply \[ \mathcal{V}_\psi(R) = \psi(\Pi_R) = \int_{\text{spec}(\Sigma)} \mathbf{1}_R(x) \, \text{d}\mu_\psi(x) = \int_{R} \text{d}\mu_\psi(x). \tag{45} \] To convert to the measure in another state \(\psi\), we use the Radon-Nikodym derivative \(\text{d}\mu_\omega/\text{d}\mu_\psi\). It is discussed at length in any measure theory text, For instance Real and Complex Analysis (1990), Walter Rudin. but for our purposes, simply converts between measures: \[ \omega(f) = \int_{\text{spec}(\Sigma)} f(x) \, \text{d}\mu_\omega(x) = \int_{\text{spec}(\Sigma)} f(x) \frac{\text{d}\mu_\omega}{\text{d}\mu_\psi}\Bigg|_x\, \text{d}\mu_\psi(x). \tag{46} \] Connes showed Noncommutative Geometry (1990), Alain Connes. Hence his self-deprecating terminology of Radon-Nikodym cocycle. that, when restricted to a masa, the cocycle (\ref{RN-point}) is precisely the square root of the Radon-Nikodym derivative, and hence, by commutativity, the volume in state \(|\psi\rangle\) is related to \(|\omega\rangle\) via \[ \omega(f) = \langle\psi|\Delta_\text{RN}^{1/2} f \Delta_\text{RN}^{1/2}|\psi\rangle \quad \Longrightarrow \mathcal{V}_\omega(R) = \langle \psi |\Delta_\text{RN}^{1/2}\Pi_R \Delta_\text{RN}^{1/2}|\psi\rangle. \tag{47}\label{swap} \] This motivates us to study the overlap of this modified Radon-Nikodym state \(\Delta_\text{RN}^{1/2}|\psi\rangle\) with \(|\psi\rangle\) itself, which is precisely the expression (\ref{RN-fid}): \[ \mathcal{F}(\omega, \psi) = \langle \psi|\Delta_\text{RN}^{1/2}|\psi\rangle = \langle\omega|\Delta_\text{RN}^{-1/2}|\omega\rangle, \tag{48} \] noting a pleasingly symmetric definition in terms of \(|\omega\rangle\) which follows immediately from (\ref{swap}), and is consistent with \(\Delta_\text{RN}^{-1} = \text{d}\mu_\psi/\text{d}\mu_\omega\). When the states are equal, \(\text{d}\mu_\omega/\text{d}\mu_\psi = I\) and hence \[ \mathcal{F}(\omega,\psi)=\langle \psi|\Delta_\text{RN}^{1/2}|\psi\rangle =1. \tag{49} \] Moreoever, since \(\langle \psi|\Delta_\text{RN}|\psi\rangle = \langle \omega|\omega\rangle = 1\), Cauchy-Schwarz shows that \[ |\mathcal{F}(\omega,\psi)|^2 = |\langle \psi |\Delta_\text{RN}^{1/2}|\psi\rangle|^2 \leq 1. \tag{50} \label{fid-bound} \] Less trivial is the statement that the inequality is strict when \(\omega \neq \psi\), just like relative entropy. The imaginary Loschmidt echo \(\mathcal{F}(\omega,\psi)\) is more commonly known as the fidelity in the quantum information literature, See for instance Quantum Computation and Quantum Information (2000), Michael Nielsen and Isaac Chuang. and is a robust measure of the difference between quantum states.

In the same way that relative entropy grounds time dilation, fidelity is the invariant which grounds modular length contraction; it is invariant under automorphisms that preserve \(|\psi\rangle\) for the same reason. In terms of (\ref{RN-fid}), and focusing on the leading terms in the \(\lambda\) integral, \[ \int \text{e}^{-\lambda/2} \, \text{d}\mu(\lambda) \lesssim 1. \tag{51} \] Interpreting \(\text{e}^{-\lambda/2}\) as a length contraction factor, it seems that moving rulers shrink on average in the same way that moving clocks slow on average, albeit with slightly different averages. But they are directly connected in the case of a single, perfectly localized eigenvalue \(\text{d}\mu(\lambda) = \delta(\lambda - \lambda_0) \,\text{d}\lambda\), where we have the striking relation \[ \mathcal{F}(\omega, \psi)^{2} = \text{e}^{-\lambda_0} = \text{e}^{-S(\omega \Vert \psi)}. \tag{52} \] When there is dispersion, Jensen's inequality is strict and they disagree, with \(\mathcal{F} > \text{e}^{-S/2}\). Thus, we can think of a well-localized spectrum as a sort of classical limit of modular relativity. In contrast, the difference \[ \mathcal{K}(\omega, \psi) = \log[\mathcal{F}(\omega,\psi)^2 \text{e}^{S(\omega\Vert \psi)}] \geq 0 \tag{53} \] represents the sum of higher cumulants (\(\mathcal{K}\) stands for “kumulant”). A novel prediction of this theory is that generically \(K >0\), though we will discuss mechanisms for classical localization of the cocycle spectrum below.

4.3 Phenomenology

Our third question regarded the connection between modular relativity and special relativity. We have been referring rather loosely to “time dilation”, “length contraction” and “boosts”. To see how this terminology holds up, we can consider the vacuum state on the Rindler wedge. The groundbreaking theorem of Bisognano and Wichmann “On the duality condition for a Hermitian scalar field” (1975), Bisognano and Wichmann. shows that the modular Hamiltonian can identified with the boost generator up to a constant, \(\Delta_\psi = \text{e}^{-2\pi K_\text{boost}}\). The rapidity is related to modular time by \(\eta = 2\pi t\), or equivalently \(\Delta_\psi^{it} = \text{e}^{-\eta K_\text{boost}}\), and diagonalizes in null coordinates \(x^\pm = t \mp x\) with \[ x^\pm \mapsto \text{e}^{\pm \eta} x^{\pm}. \tag{54} \] This suggests we identify \[| \langle\eta\rangle| = C \cdot S(\omega \Vert \psi) \tag{55} \] i.e. the average rapidity is the relative entropy, up to a prefactor, with modular time dilation in the null coordinate \(x^{\text{sgn}(\eta)}\) and modular length contraction in the complementary null coordinate \(x^{-\text{sgn}(\eta)}\). Thus, the names “time dilation” and “length contraction” might be better called modular null contraction and modular null dilation. In view of this terminology, we could call σ² the “modular null asymmetry”; “variance” is friendlier if less exact. They identify modular time as stretched along one null direction (positive rapidity) and modular space as squeezed along the other (negative rapidity). This is equivalent to \(\mathcal{K}(\omega,\psi) \geq 0\), so the identification of directions is tied to both invariants. We discuss the information-geometric aspect of this story further below.

You might wonder why our earlier arguments didn't reproduce the standard time dilation and length contraction factors. What went wrong? The short answer is that the frequencies associated to modular evolution are connected in geometric settings to boosts, and these act diagonally in the null directions. The Radon-Nikodym derivative is really the Jacobian of a change of spacetime measure, restricted to a spacelike surface. The \(\text{e}^\eta\) scaling in modular time induces a \(\text{e}^{-\eta}\) scaling in modular space, which shows up in the change of measure formula. To be clear, spacelike surfaces do not contain a null direction! The point is that the contraction is diagonal when parameterized by the appropriate null direction. Rindler space is the canonical example of this phenomenon, where the spacelike surfaces fan out from the origin and undergo negative rapidity compression. That said, we can recover the familiar Lorentz factor from basic kinematics. Recall that \(\gamma\) is related to rapidity by \[ \gamma = \cosh(\eta) = \frac{1}{2}(\text{e}^\eta + \text{e}^{-\eta}). \tag{56} \] A moving clock slows by \(\gamma\), since (setting \(x=0\), i.e. stationary in original frame) \[ t =\frac{1}{2}(x^+ + x^-) \mapsto t' = \frac{1}{2}(\text{e}^\eta + \text{e}^{-\eta})t = \gamma t. \tag{57} \] On the other hand, a moving ruler is measured to have simultaneous endpoints, or \[ \Delta t \mapsto \Delta t' = \frac{1}{2}(\text{e}^\eta + \text{e}^{-\eta})t + \frac{1}{2}(\text{e}^\eta - \text{e}^{-\eta})x = 0. \tag{58} \] We can substitute this into the expression for \(\Delta x'\):

\begin{align} \Delta x = \frac{1}{2}(\Delta x^+ -\Delta x^-) \mapsto \Delta x' & = \frac{1}{2}[\text{e}^\eta (\Delta t + \Delta x) - \text{e}^{-\eta}(\Delta t -\Delta x)] \\ & = \frac{1}{2}(\text{e}^\eta - \text{e}^{-\eta}) \Delta t + \frac{1}{2}(\text{e}^\eta + \text{e}^{-\eta}) \Delta x \\ & = \frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{-(\text{e}^\eta - \text{e}^{-\eta})^2}{(\text{e}^\eta +\text{e}^{-\eta})} + (\text{e}^\eta + \text{e}^{-\eta}) \right] \Delta x \\ & = \frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{4}{(\text{e}^\eta +\text{e}^{-\eta})}\right] \Delta x = \frac{\Delta x}{\gamma}, \end{align}

using the simultaneity condition on the third line. We therefore obtain the standard Lorentz phenomenology from the modular null scalings.

5 Examples

In this section, we explore some simple, tractable examples which ground the identification of relative entropy with (average) rapidity, and shed light on the relative modular spectrum. We first consider a finite-dimensional toy model, then a simple field theory example where we compare the vacuum state to a coherent excited state.

5.1 A toy model

Consider a commutative algebra \(\mathfrak{M}\) of mixed states, diagonal in some common basis: \[ \rho_i = \sum_{E} p_i(E) |E\rangle\langle E|. \tag{59} \] Since these commute, so the calculations become effectively classical. Suppose the algebra acts on a Hilbert space \(\mathscr{H}\), with commutant (i.e. operators which commute with each element) \(\mathfrak{M}'\). The theory of finite-dimensional modular flow tells us that \[ \Delta_{i} = \rho_i \otimes \rho_i^{-1}, \quad \Delta_{i|j} = \rho_i \otimes \rho_j^{-1}, \quad [D\rho_i: D\rho_j]_{-i} = \rho_i\rho_j^{-1} \otimes e. \tag{60} \] Thus, evaluated at a fixed energy \(E\), the relative modular operator is just the likelihood ratio, and the cocycle spectral distribution is the log likelihood: \[ [D\rho_i: D\rho_j]_{-i} = \frac{p_{i}(E)}{p_{j}(E)}, \quad \tag{61} \lambda(E) = -\log \Delta_{i|j}(E) = \log \left[\frac{p_{j}(E)}{p_{i}(E)}\right]. \] The relative entropy is the expected log likelihood or KL divergence: \[ S(\rho_i \Vert \rho_j) = \mathbb{E}_{j} \left[\log \frac{p_j}{p_i}\right] = \mathbb{E}_{j} [\lambda(E)]. \tag{62} \] Thus, \(\lambda(E)\) is the spectral density of the cocycle Hamiltonian, \(S(\rho_i\Vert\rho_j)\) is the averaged spectral density, and treating Hamiltonian as a boost generator, the relative entropy is the average rapidity. At the RN point, \([D\rho_i: D\rho_j]_{-i/2} = \rho_j^{1/2}\rho_i^{-1/2}\), and hence \[ \mathcal{F}(\rho_i, \rho_j)=\mbox{Tr}\big[\rho_j [D\rho_i: D\rho_j]_{-i/2}\big] = \mbox{Tr}[\sqrt{\rho_i}\sqrt{\rho_j}] = \sum_E \sqrt{p_i(E)p_j(E)}, \tag{63} \] the classical Bhattacharyya distance. This can also be written \[ \mathcal{F}(\rho_i, \rho_j) = \mathbb{E}_{j}\left[\text{e}^{-\lambda/2}\right] \tag{64} \] in accord with the integral (\ref{RN-fid}). To explore localization of the spectrum, note that, by Taylor expand the exponential and the log, we have \[ \log \mathcal{F} = \log \mathbb{E}_j \left[\text{e}^{-\lambda/2}\right] = -\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_j[\lambda] + \frac{1}{8}\sigma^2_j[\lambda] - \frac{1}{48}\kappa_3 + \cdots, \tag{65} \] or equivalently \[ \mathcal{K} = \log (\mathcal{F}^2\text{e}^{S}) = \frac{1}{4}\sigma^2 + \mathcal{O}(\kappa_3),\tag{66} \] so \(\mathcal{K}\) is indeed a sum of higher cumulants, as claimed earlier. As a special case, we can consider thermal density matrices with respect to the same Hamiltonian, \[ \rho_i = \frac{1}{Z(\beta_i)} \text{e}^{-\beta_i H}, \quad Z(\beta_i) = \mbox{Tr}[\text{e}^{-\beta_i H}]. \tag{67} \] In this case, a short calculation shows that \[ \sigma^2_j[\lambda] = (\beta_j - \beta_i) \sigma^2_j[E] = (\beta_j - \beta_i) \partial_\beta^2 \log Z(\beta)\bigg|_{\beta=\beta_j} = \frac{\Delta\beta \cdot C_v(T_j)}{k_B \beta_j^2}. \tag{68} \] Thus, the cocycle spectrum is effectively localized (small variance) provided the inverse temperature is much larger than the heat capacity: \[ C_v(T_j) \ll \frac{k_B\beta^2}{\Delta\beta} = \frac{1}{T_j(1 + T_j/T_i)}. \tag{69} \] For a finite-level system, we typically have This is called the Schottky anomaly. \(C_v(T) \propto T^{-2}\), so localization occurs in the high-temperature limit.

5.2 Coherent states

While the toy model illustrates the basic structure, we now turn to a quantum field theory example which exhibits the full richness of modular relativity in a geometric setting. Consider the right Rindler wedge \[ W = \{(t, x_1, \mathbf{x}) \in \mathbb{R}^{0, d} : x_1 > |t|\}, \tag{70} \] and the associated algebra of local excitations for a Hermitian field \[ \mathfrak{A}(W) = \{\phi(x) : x \in W\}. \tag{71} \] If \(|\psi\rangle\) is the Minkowski vacuum (the unique state invariant under Poincaré transformations) restricted to \(W\), then by the Reeh-Schlieder theorem “Bemerkungen zur Unitäräquivalenz von Lorentzinvarianten Feldern” (1961), Helmut Reeh and Siegfried Schlieder. The technical definition of the vacuum is rather involved, but our caricature is a common one. See “An algebraic approach to quantum field theory” (1964), Rudolf Haag and Daniel Kastler. it is cyclic and separating. Bisognano and Wichmann proved that the modular Hamiltonian is precisely the boost generator up to a reparameterization factor of \(2\pi\): \[ K_\psi = 2\pi K_\text{boost}, \tag{72} \] with parameter \(\eta =2\pi t\) the rapidity. A localized coherent excitation is produced by a Weyl unitary \(W(f)\) acting on the vacuum, \(|\omega_f\rangle = W(f)|\psi\rangle\); this is also cyclic and separating. Assuming for simplicity that the smearing function \(f\) is localized to the right wedge. Then Casini, Grillo and Pontello show that “Relative entropy for coherent states from Araki formula” (2019), Horacio Casini, Sergio Grillo, and Diego Pontello. \[ S(\omega_f \Vert \psi) = 2\pi \langle f, k_1 f\rangle_{\mathscr{H}_\text{1P}}, \tag{73} \] where \(k_1\) is the one-particle boost generator and \(\mathscr{H}_\text{1P}\) is the one-particle Hilbert space (we omit the subscript from now on). If we expand the spectrum of the one-particle boost generator as \[ k_1 = \int \lambda \, \text{d}\varsigma(\lambda) \tag{75} \] and substitute into the relative entropy, we obtain \[ S(\omega_f \Vert \psi) = 2\pi \langle f, f\rangle\int \lambda \, \mathrm{d}\mu(\lambda), \quad \text{d}\mu(\lambda) = \frac{\langle f, \text{d}\varsigma(\lambda) f\rangle}{\langle f, f\rangle}. \tag{76} \] Again, we have a direct connection to the spectrum of boosts, up to a prefactor \(2\pi \langle f, f\rangle\). If we set \(\text{d}\mu(\lambda) = \delta(\lambda-\lambda_0) \,\text{d}\lambda\), we find \(S(\omega_f \Vert \psi) = 2\pi \langle f, f\rangle \lambda_0\). Casini, Grillo and Pontello also prove that the Connes cocycle takes the form \[ [D\omega_f : D\psi]_t = W(f)\sigma^t_\psi [W(f)^*] = W(f)W(f_t), \tag{77} \] where \(f_t\) is a boosted smearing function. Taking the vacuum overlaps, Cadamuro and Del Vecchio compute the closed form “Relative entropy of coherent states on general CCR algebras” (2022), Daniela Cadamuro and Simone Del Vecchio. \[ \bigg| \langle\psi |[D\omega_f : D\psi]_{-i/2}|\psi\rangle\bigg|^2 = \exp\left[-\langle\Delta_\psi^{1/2} f - f, \Delta_\psi^{1/2} f - f\rangle\right]. \tag{78} \] Specializing to the Rindler wedge, we have \(\Delta_\psi^{1/2} = \text{e}^{-\pi k_1}\) and for a small, localized eigenpacket (\(\Delta_{\psi}^{1/2} - e \approx -\pi k_1\)) we get \[ \bigg| \langle\psi |[D\omega_f : D\psi]_{-i/2}|\psi\rangle\bigg|^2 \approx \text{e}^{-2\pi \langle f, f\rangle \lambda_0}, \tag{79} \] which precisely agrees with \(\text{e}^{-S}\), as we expect for the well-localized limit. Note that for this to occur, we need localization at small eigenvalues, otherwise corrections (corresponding to higher terms in the \(\lambda\)-integral (\ref{RN-fid})) spoil the agreement. We therefore predict nonzero \(\mathcal{K}\) at high boost.

6 Open questions

Below, we discuss some unresolved problems we hope to take up in future work: localization, the nature of observers, the quantum information geometry, and extensions to curvature.

In our examples, we identified two regimes where localization of the modular spectrum held: the high-temperature limit of a finite-dimensional system, with states sharing an eigenbasis; and for coherent excitations in Rindler space with a narrow boost eigenpacket at small average value of \(\lambda\). In this case, the kumulant function \(\mathcal{K} \approx 0\) and the fidelity is directly related to the relative entropy. There are two sorts of corrections to this picture: higher cumulants of the modular spectrum, and Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff corrections to the Radon-Nikodym density. These can loosely be viewed as stochastic and quantum effects respectively. This leads to the question:

How does classical spacetime suppress corrections to modular localization?

One possibility is that the relative modular Hamiltonian acts as a pointer basis for decoherence, “Decoherence, einselection, and the quantum origins of the classical” (2001), Wojciech Zurek. but this would only suppress quantum corrections, not stochastic.

Modular relativity involves a notion of time given by a state dependent modular flow \(\sigma^t_\psi\), and a notion of space given by a maximal commutative subalgebra \(\Sigma\). Although we discussed an observer-independent \(\Sigma\) for simplicity, it seems natural to promote this to a function of the observer, \(\Sigma_\psi\). Then the accessible algebra accessible to an observer is This is a fancy way of saying the smallest von Neumann algebra containing the image of the commutative subalgebra under modular flow. \[ \mathfrak{M}_\psi = [\sigma^\mathbb{R}_\psi(\Sigma_\psi)]''. \tag{80} \] This is not always the whole algebra \(\mathfrak{M}\), and in fact, \(\Sigma\) need not be maximal with respect to \(\mathfrak{M}\). In the Rindler wedge \(W\), for instance, we take a spatial slice \(\Sigma_\psi = \mathbb{R}_{>0}\) and modular flow given by hyperbolic trajectories. The accessible algebra in this case is simply the wedge algebra \(\mathfrak{M}_\psi = \mathfrak{A}(W)\). Although \(\Sigma_\psi\) is not maximal with respect to \(\mathfrak{M}\), it is maximal with respect to \(\mathfrak{M}_\psi\). This suggests that we require \(\Sigma_\psi\) to be a masa within \(\mathfrak{M}_\psi\); this may seem circular, but the point is that completion into a von Neumann algebra shouldn't be needed to fill in holes in \(\Sigma_\psi\). This is a start, but can we constrain this more strongly?

What is the nature of the spatial system \(\Sigma_\psi\) associated to an observer \(|\psi\rangle\)?

One possible type of constraint is thermodynamic in origin. A large part of Connes and Rovelli's motivation for using modular time was its thermal nature, since each state is KMS, by definition, with respect to its induced modular flow. One issue with the TTH in this context is that this is almost too strong; each observer appears to be in equilibrium by definition, but it is precisely the non-equilibrium effects which seem to account for the arrow of time. Thus:

Does modular relativity encode non-equilbrium interactions between observers?

The interplay between relative entropy and fidelity is a natural place to seek such effects. Relative entropy governs boosts, while fidelity controls the Radon-Nikodym Jacobian. At the same time, relative entropy governs the well-known Fisher information metric for quantum and classical states. See for instance “Statistical distance and the geometry of quantum states” (2001), Samuel Braunstein and Carlton Caves. Given that observers are states in our formulation, this raises the question:

What is the Fisher information metric associated with many observers?

Relating this metric to the emergence of a familiar spacetime, and the role of fidelity (particularly for nonzero kumulant) are natural sequels. Regarding the former, of particular interest is general relativity, which generalizes special relativity to situations to a manifold with local Lorentz symmetry. This parallels the development of algebraic quantum field theory, which generalizes a single von Neumann algebra to a net of local algebras. Hence:

Does modular relativity for a net of local algebras lead to general relativity?

This is closely related to Borchers' program for recovering Lorentz symmetry, On Revolutionizing of Quantum Field Theory with Tomita's Modular Theory (1999), H.J. Borchers. but applied to local Lorentz symmetry. Given that observers may exhibit nontrivial thermal physics, it's natural to wonder how this ties into proposals For instance “Thermodynamics of Spacetime: The Einstein Equation of State” (2001), Ted Jacobson. relating general relativity to thermodynamics.

7 Conclusion

Einstein’s late suspicion was not merely that the continuum is “too big,” but that it is structurally mismatched to what quantum theory seems to demand: finite information about finite systems, encoded algebraically rather than geometrically. In the opening vignette, we imagined him drifting—perhaps unwillingly—toward von Neumann’s “rings of operators,” not as a mathematician chasing abstraction, but as a physicist chasing a language in which reality is what can be acted on and measured. This essay took that premise seriously: start with the algebra of operations \(\mathfrak{M}\), and ask whether the familiar furniture of spacetime can be recovered as derived structure rather than assumed background.

What emerged is a remarkably tight triad. Space appears as the maximal commutative shadow of \(\mathfrak{M}\): a (possibly observer-dependent) “spatial” subalgebra whose spectrum behaves like a coordinate space, though one that naturally trades point-locality for projection-locality. Time appears not as an external parameter but as the intrinsic one-parameter automorphism group \(\sigma_\psi^t\) associated to a state \(|\psi\rangle\) via Tomita–Takesaki theory: a canonical dynamical flow that exists precisely because the algebra is noncommutative and the state is sufficiently rich (cyclic and separating). And finally, observers appear not as extra ingredients but as states themselves, with relations between observers encoded by the relative modular operator and Connes’ cocycle—the minimal “bridge” allowing one state’s modular world to be compared to another’s.

Once one admits observers-as-states, the familiar relativity questions can be asked in a purely information-theoretic dialect. The cocycle evaluated near \(t=0\) produces relative entropy, which we interpreted as an intrinsic “clock comparison”: a modular analogue of time dilation grounded not in the invariance of \(c\), but in the invariance of distinguishability under symmetries of the reference state. Passing from real time to the Radon–Nikodym point \(t=-i/2\) produces fidelity, which we read as the spatial analogue: a Jacobian-like factor converting measures on (commutative) spatial slices, thus a modular analogue of length contraction. In the well-localized limit these invariants lock together (\(\mathcal{F}^2 \approx e^{-S}\)), while dispersion in the relative modular spectrum produces a genuinely new correction term \(\mathcal{K}\)—a quantitative reminder that “classical kinematics” is the special case where the cocycle spectrum behaves almost like a single rapidity.

The Rindler example shows that this is not merely metaphor. In the wedge algebra, Bisognano–Wichmann identifies the modular Hamiltonian of the vacuum with the Lorentz boost generator (up to \(2\pi\)), so modular time becomes rapidity, and the “dilation/contraction” language becomes literally geometric, i.e. diagonal in null coordinates. The coherent-state comparison then supplies a second anchor: relative entropy and the cocycle admit closed expressions in terms of the one-particle boost generator and boosted smearings, making the information-theoretic invariants computable and the localization discussion concrete rather than rhetorical. The upshot is that special-relativistic phenomenology is not imposed, but appears as the geometric face of modular structure in a setting where the algebra already knows about causal localization.

So what becomes of Einstein’s pessimism? If the continuum is indeed the wrong primitive, then the “purely algebraic theory” he regarded as unknowable may not look like a discrete lattice, or a combinatorial replacement for \(\mathbb{R}^4\), but rather like a theory in which spacetime is the macroscopic organization of information: commutative subalgebras as spatial surfaces, modular flows as temporal directions, and observer-comparisons governed by invariants (relative entropy, fidelity) that do not care about coordinates. In that light, the sea-image from the introduction is apt: one does not break the continuum; one quietly surrounds it, until geometry is no longer foundational but emergent.

The open questions in §5 are therefore not epilogues but the real beginning: what selects the “right” \(\Sigma_\psi\) for an observer; how classical localization suppresses cumulants and Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff corrections; how the Fisher metric on state space becomes the seed of spacetime geometry; and whether a net of local algebras forces a general-relativistic analogue of local Lorentz symmetry. If modular relativity is more than a parable, its next tests will come precisely where Einstein’s instincts were sharpest: in the tension between locality, covariance, and thermodynamics, where “time” is not a background parameter but something an observer extracts from the algebra of the world.
